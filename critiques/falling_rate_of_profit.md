# Praxeological Critique of Marx's Tendency of the Rate of Profit to Fall (TRPF)

## 1. Original Argument and Theoretical Explanation

In Volume III of *Das Kapital* (1894), Karl Marx formulated the **Tendency of the Rate of Profit to Fall (TRPF)**, claiming it constituted the principal mechanical contradiction driving capitalist production to recurrent crises and secular collapse:

1. **The Rate of Profit Formula:**
   Marx expressed the economy-wide rate of profit $r$ as the ratio of surplus value $s$ to total capital outlay, $c + v$:

$$
r = \frac{s}{c + v} = \frac{s/v}{c/v + 1} = \frac{s'}{\theta + 1}
$$

   where:
   - $c$ is **constant capital** (monetary expenditures on physical machinery, plant, and raw materials).
   - $v$ is **variable capital** (monetary wages paid to living labor).
   - $s$ is **surplus value** (value generated by living labor beyond the cost of its subsistence).
   - $s' = s/v$ is the **rate of surplus value** (rate of exploitation).
   - $\theta = c/v$ is the **organic composition of capital**.
2. **The Inevitable Secular Mechanization:**
   Marx argued that competitive pressure forces capitalists to invest in labor-saving machinery, increasing constant capital $c$ faster than variable capital $v$, causing the organic composition of capital $\theta$ to rise secularly across all industries.
3. **The Living Labor Dogma and Crisis Mechanism:**
   Under the Labor Theory of Value, constant capital is "dead labor" that merely transfers its existing value to the product without generating new surplus value. Only living labor $(v)$ creates surplus value $(s)$. Because mechanization lowers the relative proportion of living labor employed across the system, the denominator $\theta + 1$ grows indefinitely while the sole generator of surplus value shrinks relative to total capital outlay, $c + v$. Consequently, the profit rate $r$ must inevitably fall, choking off the incentive to invest, precipitating mass bankruptcies, and forcing periodic crises of overaccumulation.

## 2. Formal Definitions of the Target Doctrine

To evaluate whether Marx's thesis holds mathematically and economically, we formulate the target doctrine's categories within the monetary cost and production framework developed in Chapter 9:

### Definition 1 (Factor Outlays, Revenue, and Organic Composition)
For an entrepreneur evaluating a production technique $\alpha \in \mathcal{A}$ under prevailing market factor prices $P$, partition monetary input outlays $K(\alpha, P) = C_P(\alpha)$ into two discrete components:
1. **Constant Capital Outlay** $(c)$:

$$
c(\alpha, P) = \sum_{m \in \mathcal{M}_{\text{cap}}} P_m \cdot u(\alpha, m)
$$

2. **Variable Capital Outlay** $(v)$:

$$
v(\alpha, P) = P_{\text{wage}} \cdot u(\alpha, m_{\text{labor}})
$$

3. **Total Capital Outlay** $(K)$:

$$
K(\alpha, P) = c(\alpha, P) + v(\alpha, P)
$$

4. **Gross Output Revenue** $(R)$:

$$
R(\alpha, P) = P_{\text{out}} \cdot y(\alpha, m_{\text{out}})
$$

5. **Surplus Value and Rate of Profit** ( $s$ and $r$):
   Net monetary return is $\Pi(\alpha, P) = R(\alpha, P) - K(\alpha, P)$. Marx's rate of profit is the return-to-cost ratio:

$$
r(\alpha, P) = \frac{\Pi(\alpha, P)}{K(\alpha, P)} = \frac{R(\alpha, P) - (c(\alpha, P) + v(\alpha, P))}{c(\alpha, P) + v(\alpha, P)}
$$

6. **Organic Composition of Capital** $(\theta)$:
   The factor outlay ratio between constant and variable capital, compared across two techniques by the cross-multiplication:

$$
\theta(\alpha_{\text{new}}) > \theta(\alpha_{\text{old}}) \iff c(\alpha_{\text{old}}) \cdot v(\alpha_{\text{new}}) < c(\alpha_{\text{new}}) \cdot v(\alpha_{\text{old}})
$$

### Definition 2 (Viable Mechanizing Innovation)
An entrepreneur adopts a new mechanizing technique $\alpha_{\text{new}}$ over an existing viable baseline $\alpha_{\text{old}}$ satisfying $K(\alpha_{\text{old}}) \le R(\alpha_{\text{old}})$ if and only if it is purposefully viable:
1. **Capital-Labor Substitution:** Constant capital outlay rises and variable labor outlay falls:

$$
c(\alpha_{\text{old}}, P) < c(\alpha_{\text{new}}, P) \quad \wedge \quad v(\alpha_{\text{new}}, P) < v(\alpha_{\text{old}}, P)
$$

2. **Strict Unit Cost Reduction:** Total monetary production cost strictly falls at current market prices:

$$
K(\alpha_{\text{new}}, P) < K(\alpha_{\text{old}}, P)
$$

3. **Output Revenue Preservation:** The gross output produced commands at least the prior gross revenue:

$$
R(\alpha_{\text{old}}, P) \le R(\alpha_{\text{new}}, P)
$$

## 3. Viable Mechanization Strictly Increases the Profit Rate

**Proposition.** Under Marx's own definitions, whenever an entrepreneur adopts a viable mechanizing innovation that raises the organic composition of capital, with $\theta(\alpha_{\text{new}}) > \theta(\alpha_{\text{old}})$, while reducing total unit production cost, with $K(\alpha_{\text{new}}) < K(\alpha_{\text{old}})$, both the net monetary surplus and the exact rate of profit strictly increase:

$$
s(\alpha_{\text{new}}) > s(\alpha_{\text{old}}) \quad \wedge \quad r(\alpha_{\text{new}}) > r(\alpha_{\text{old}})
$$

**Proof.**
1. By Definition 1, $s = R - K$. Since $R(\alpha_{\text{new}}) \ge R(\alpha_{\text{old}})$ and $K(\alpha_{\text{new}}) < K(\alpha_{\text{old}})$, subtracting the strictly smaller cost from the weakly larger revenue yields:

$$
s(\alpha_{\text{new}}) = R(\alpha_{\text{new}}) - K(\alpha_{\text{new}}) > R(\alpha_{\text{old}}) - K(\alpha_{\text{old}}) = s(\alpha_{\text{old}})
$$

2. By [Proposition 9.20](../natural_language/09_money_prices_and_calculation.md#proposition-920-cost-reducing-factor-substitution-raises-net-return-and-return-ratio) from Chapter 9, substituting factor outlays while strictly lowering total cost yields a strictly higher return-to-cost ratio:

$$
s(\alpha_{\text{old}}) \cdot K(\alpha_{\text{new}}) < s(\alpha_{\text{new}}) \cdot K(\alpha_{\text{new}}) \le s(\alpha_{\text{new}}) \cdot K(\alpha_{\text{old}})
$$

Dividing both sides by the positive product $K(\alpha_{\text{old}}) \cdot K(\alpha_{\text{new}})$ proves $r(\alpha_{\text{new}}) > r(\alpha_{\text{old}})$. $\blacksquare$

Marx's claim that viable mechanization depresses the rate of profit is an internal algebraic contradiction within his own formula.

## 4. Resemblance to Okishio's Theorem and Its Praxeological Boundaries

The theorem on viable mechanization provides a praxeological counterpart to the celebrated theorem of **Nobuo Okishio (1961)**. 

Okishio analyzed the general equilibrium response of relative prices when a viable cost-reducing technique spreads across the economy under constant real wages:
1. When mechanization occurs in capital-goods industries, the replacement cost of machinery and raw materials falls, so that $`P'_m \le P_m`$.
2. When mechanization occurs in wage-goods industries, the cost of the physical consumption basket falls, cheapening the monetary wage required to command the same real wage, so that $`P'_{\text{wage}} \le P_{\text{wage}}`$.
3. Applying [Proposition 9.20](../natural_language/09_money_prices_and_calculation.md#proposition-920-cost-reducing-factor-substitution-raises-net-return-and-return-ratio), downward adjustments in factor reproduction prices, with $K' \le K_{\text{new}} < K_{\text{old}}$, strictly preserve unit cost savings, proving that general equilibrium profit rates cannot fall, with $r' \ge r_{\text{new}} > r_{\text{old}}$.

**Praxeological Limitations of Okishio's Model:**
While Okishio successfully refuted Marx within Marxian/Sraffian terms, his own mathematical model suffers from neoclassical shortcomings:
- Okishio assumes a static, timeless system of simultaneous equations without money, where goods reproduce themselves instantaneously.
- The Lean model includes intertemporal choices over a branching history. Its unified time-preference synthesis (Chapter 7, [Theorem 7.16](../natural_language/07_time_preference_interest_and_capital.md#theorem-716-unified-positive-time-preference-saving-resolution-and-originary-interest-synthesis)) proves universal dated preference over fixed ends under open horizons and positive originary interest ($\delta \ge 1$) under explicit discounting and control premises. Bridging these discrete loan and saving results to a macroeconomic return floor on physical production techniques requires the specific capital and market premises stated in Chapters 7 and 9.

## 5. Doctrinal Fallacies and the Theory of Crises

### The Numerator/Denominator Fallacy: Value vs. Physical Mass
Marx's assertion that the organic composition of capital, $\theta = c/v$, must rise secularly confuses physical quantities of machinery with economic value:
- An enterprise may deploy ten times more physical machines per worker.
- However, if technological advances reduce the labor and resources required to manufacture those machines, the total monetary value of constant capital per worker, $c/v$, can fall rather than rise.
- The cheapening of constant capital is not an accidental counteracting tendency; it is the direct and necessary consequence of the very technological productivity that motivates mechanization.

### Capital Goods as Physical Factors vs. Sterile "Dead Labor"
The foundation of the TRPF rests on the dogma that capital equipment is "dead labor" incapable of generating surplus value.

In praxeological reality, capital goods are productive physical factors that alter physical causality (Chapter 1 and Chapter 4). Combining labor with tools yields more physical output per unit of input than manual labor alone:

$$
y(\alpha_{\text{mechanized}}, m_{\text{out}}) > y(\alpha_{\text{manual}}, m_{\text{out}})
$$

Factors of production do not embody value; value is imputed backward from the subjective appraisal of consumer ends (Chapter 4). Capital goods contribute to the satisfaction of ends by increasing physical output, expanding the feasible action menu, and economizing on human labor.

### The True Cause of Crises: Fiduciary Credit Distortion vs. Falling Profit
Marx attributed periodic industrial crises to the secular exhaustion of the profit rate.

As demonstrated in Chapter 13, systemic economic crises are not caused by falling profit rates resulting from mechanization. They are caused by **fiduciary credit expansion**:
- When the banking system expands credit unbacked by voluntary saving, it suppresses loan interest rates below the natural originary rate.
- This distorts entrepreneurial appraisals, inducing an economy-wide cluster of malinvestments in overly roundabout, long-horizon capital projects.
- When the complementary physical factors required to complete these projects are physically exhausted, the boom collapses into a crisis of factor starvation and capital abandonment.
Crises are the result of monetary distortion of price signals in branching time, not an inherent self-destruction of capital mechanization.
